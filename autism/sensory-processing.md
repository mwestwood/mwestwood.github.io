# Understanding Sensory Processing in Autism

For many autistic people, sensory experiences aren't just more intense — they're processed differently at a neurological level.

---

## The Basics

The brain constantly receives input from eight sensory systems:

| Sense | What it Detects |
|:---|:---|
| **Visual** | Light, color, movement |
| **Auditory** | Sound, frequency, volume |
| **Tactile** | Touch, pressure, texture, temperature |
| **Olfactory** | Smell |
| **Gustatory** | Taste |
| **Vestibular** | Balance and spatial orientation |
| **Proprioceptive** | Body position and muscle feedback |
| **Interoceptive** | Internal body signals (hunger, pain, heartbeat) |

In autism, the filtering and integration of these signals can differ significantly — sometimes leading to **hypersensitivity** (over-responsiveness) or **hyposensitivity** (under-responsiveness), often in the same person across different senses.

---

## Hypersensitivity vs. Hyposensitivity

{: .highlight }
> **Hypersensitive:** A seam in a sock feels like a hot wire. A crowded hallway is deafening. Fluorescent lights flicker visibly at 60Hz.

{: .note }
> **Hyposensitive:** Doesn't feel pain from an injury that would stop most people. Needs heavy pressure or movement to feel regulated. May not notice when hungry or cold.

Both can be present in the same person — hypersensitive to sound but hyposensitive to pain, for example.

---

## Sensory Overload

When sensory input exceeds the brain's capacity to process it, **overload** can occur. This isn't a behavioral choice or manipulation — it's a neurological event.

Signs may include:

- Covering ears, eyes, or rocking
- Becoming non-verbal or minimally verbal
- Emotional dysregulation (meltdown or shutdown)
- Physical withdrawal from the environment

{: .warning }
> A **meltdown** is an involuntary response to overload — not a tantrum. A **shutdown** is the same overwhelm expressed inward as withdrawal rather than outward distress.

---

## What Helps

### Environmental accommodations

- Reduce fluorescent or flickering lighting (use lamps or natural light)
- Allow headphones or earplugs in loud environments
- Offer clothing without scratchy tags, seams, or tight fits
- Provide a quiet retreat space when environments are overwhelming

### Sensory regulation tools

- **Weighted blankets or vests** — deep pressure can calm the nervous system
- **Fidgets** — provide proprioceptive input that supports focus
- **Movement breaks** — jumping, spinning, or swinging can be regulating, not disruptive
- **Chewable jewelry** — safe oral sensory input

### Understanding first

Before assuming a behavior is problematic, ask: *could this be a sensory response?* Rocking, humming, hand-flapping, pacing — these are often **self-regulation strategies**, not behaviors to eliminate.

---

## A Note on Language

Some people prefer **identity-first language** ("autistic person") while others prefer **person-first language** ("person with autism"). When in doubt, ask the individual or follow their lead.
